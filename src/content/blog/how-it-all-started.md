Like many people my age, I grew up playing video games. Over the years I owned a PlayStation 1, PlayStation 2, Game Boy Advance, and eventually an Xbox 360. We also had a desktop computer that my cousin built for our family when Windows 7 was still relatively new. Technology was always around me, but I never really understood how it worked behind the scenes.

That changed in December 2015.

After months of saving money, I purchased roughly $500 worth of computer parts and set out to build my first desktop computer. Armed with countless YouTube tutorials and hours of Google searches, I was convinced I was ready. Looking back, I probably knew less than I thought I did, but the excitement of building something myself outweighed any uncertainty.

At first, the computer did not radically change my daily routine. My friends and I still played most of our games on consoles, and I was the only one in the group with a desktop. Most of my computer usage was limited to schoolwork, solo gaming, and watching movies.

Then I discovered microelectronics.

The topic fascinated me. The next time my family passed a RadioShack, I convinced them to stop so I could buy an Arduino Micro and a starter kit filled with sensors, LEDs, motors, and other components. Soon after, I picked up a few beginner electronics books to learn how circuits worked and to better understand the underlying concepts and formulas.

I loved every minute of it.

I began creating small projects with my Arduino, programming it to perform simple tasks such as turning on an LED when a light sensor detected a certain amount of light. These projects were not groundbreaking, but they taught me something important: programming was fun. There were plenty of frustrating moments and hours spent troubleshooting, but every obstacle pushed me to learn something new. For the first time, I found myself genuinely enjoying the process of solving technical problems.

As my interest in technology continued to grow, my high school began offering more computer-related courses. I enrolled in Computer Programming and Computer Concepts, joined the Computer Science Club, and immersed myself in every technology-focused opportunity I could find.

Outside the classroom, I wrote essays on cyber attacks, cybersecurity, and the growing importance of cyber warfare. I also took an Architectural Design course that used CAD software to create floor plans and technical diagrams. If a course involved technology in any way, I wanted to be part of it.

Despite my growing passion for technology, I entered high school with a different goal in mind: joining the Army. For years, it was all I wanted to do. That never changed, but my priorities began to evolve. As I progressed through high school, I realized that I wanted an education as well. To keep my options open, I enrolled in two dual-enrollment college courses.

By my senior year, I still had not chosen a college. I knew one thing for certain: I wanted to attend a military college. My goal was to earn a degree, commission as a Second Lieutenant, and ultimately serve in the Army's Cyber branch.

I visited Virginia Military Institute, The Citadel, and the University of North Georgia. In the end, I only submitted one college application. My thinking was simple: if I was accepted, I would attend. If I was not, then I would enlist in the Army and let fate take its course.

A few months later, I received my acceptance letter.

In January 2018, I began my college journey at the University of North Georgia.

Looking back, it is easy to see that building my first computer was the spark that started everything. What began as a gaming hobby evolved into an interest in electronics, programming, cybersecurity, and ultimately a career path that continues to shape my life today.
